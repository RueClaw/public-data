## Article text

There's a version of your job where you don't write most of the code anymore. You describe what needs to happen, something else does it overnight, and in the morning you read what it did and approve or reject it. Not a demo, not a toy, but a real setup that handles your inbox, your tickets, your routine fixes and your research while you're asleep.
Everything needed for that arrived in the last three weeks. OpenAI launched Dots, agents that keep working between conversations instead of stopping when you close the tab, and they run on GPT-6 Astra with about a million tokens of memory. Alongside it came GPT-6.1 Sol at a fifth of the price. And two weeks before that, a small company called TypeSafe released the strangest piece of the puzzle: a model that can't write a single word and only makes decisions, for $0.042 per million.
That last number is what makes 24/7 realistic for one person. A million small decisions cost about $42. Those same decisions handled by a frontier model would run into the thousands.
Here's the full build, explained simply enough that you can follow it even if you've never built an agent before.
What actually changed
Regular ChatGPT is a conversation. You ask, it answers, it stops. Close the tab and the work ends there.
 
A Dot works the other way around. You don't give it a question, you give it a responsibility.
help.openai.com/en/articles/20001530-getting-started-with-your-dot
OpenAI describes it as an always-on agent that keeps making progress between conversations, with its own cloud computer, access to your connected apps, and a memory that builds up over time.
 
The simplest version: ChatGPT waits for a prompt, a Dot owns a job.
That's the whole shift in your role. You stop being the person who types every instruction, and become the person who defines the job, sets the limits and reviews the output.
The engine, and what it costs
The model underneath is GPT-6 Astra.
developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-6-astra
 
It holds about 1.05 million tokens of context, which is a stack of several long books, and can write up to 128,000 tokens in one answer. You can also dial its reasoning effort from low to maximum depending on how hard the task is.

Sol launched alongside Dots with the same enormous memory at a fifth of Astra's price, aimed at serious work that doesn't need the very top of the range.
Astra is worth every cent when the job is genuinely hard. Reading forty documents and writing a proper summary. Debugging a tangled codebase. Planning something complicated. That's exactly what a frontier model is for, and nothing cheap replaces it there.

This is where solo setups usually die. The agent works, the bill arrives, and the whole thing gets switched off.
The model that can't write
TypeSafe's launch is the strangest of the year, because the entire pitch is about what the model can't do.

They describe Jev as a System One model: a situation goes in, a typed decision comes out, and no free text is ever generated. It can't write, can't code, can't chat and can't produce a report.

Putting the whole thing together
One thing to be clear about before the diagram: OpenAI and TypeSafe haven't announced any partnership, and Dots don't ship with Jev inside. This is an architecture you assemble yourself from tools both companies have published, and every piece of it exists today.
The core idea is to stop asking the expensive model what to do next, and start asking the cheap one.
 
Think of a hospital. The receptionist doesn't perform surgery, but she decides who needs the surgeon, so the surgeon spends his entire day on patients who actually need him. Jev is the receptionist, Sol is the regular doctor, Astra is the specialist you call for the hard cases.
This isn't theoretical either.

The part that makes supervising possible
There's a bigger reason to care about the decision layer, and it has nothing to do with money.
A chatbot writes an email. A Dot sends it. The moment an AI stops producing text and starts taking actions, a mistake is no longer a bad paragraph you can ignore. It's a deleted record, a wrong payment, a message to a client you can't take back.
 
A check that costs a fraction of a cent can sit in front of every single action the agent takes, which is something you'd never do with a $10 model. That's what turns "it runs while I sleep" from a terrifying sentence into a workable one.
But there's a limit worth stating plainly. LangChain currently has an open issue about this exact pattern, where under a certain ordering of components a modified action could slip past the check. That's not a reason to avoid the pattern, it's a reason to remember the rule:
 
For sorting spam, a confidence score is plenty. For deleting data, moving money or anything touching a customer's contract, a percentage should never be the final word. Hard limits belong in ordinary code that the model has no power to override, and your approval queue is the last gate.
What your actual day looks like
At 07:00 you open your laptop and the night's work is already done. 400 overnight messages were sorted by Jev for about two cents, Sol drafted replies to the dozen that needed one, and Astra was never switched on.
 
You read eight flagged items and twelve drafts. That's your morning, and it takes fifteen minutes instead of two hours.
At 11:00 something genuinely hard arrives. A 90-page contract with a request for a risk summary. Jev rates it as complex, escalates straight to Astra, and the expensive model earns its price on the one task that actually deserves it.
At 16:00 the agent wants to send a payment reminder to a customer. Jev marks it medium risk, code checks it against your rules, and it lands in your approval queue instead of going out on its own. You glance at it, approve, and move on.
Over a month the same amount of work gets done, but the frontier model switches on for a handful of moments instead of thousands of them. And your job has quietly changed from doing the work to checking it.
Start with one boring decision that happens hundreds of times a day. That's always where the savings hide, and it's also the safest place to learn how the system behaves.
What each piece solves
 
Dots solve persistence, because the work no longer stops when you close the laptop. Astra solves intelligence. Sol makes that intelligence affordable for everyday work. Jev solves the thing nobody had a name for until now, which is the economics of deciding. And code stays the only thing with real authority.
The future agent probably isn't one giant model running flat out forever. It's a small hierarchy that knows exactly when intelligence is worth paying for, and keeps the expensive brain switched off the rest of the time.
Most people will hand their always-on agent the biggest model available, get a surprising bill at the end of the month, and switch the whole thing off. A few will build the ladder first, run the same agent for a fraction of the cost, and wake up every morning to work that's already done.
The pieces are all public. The documentation is all free. The only thing between you and supervising instead of typing is one weekend.
You build your own life - so choose the right path.
