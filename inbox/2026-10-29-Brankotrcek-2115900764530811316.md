I opened the terminal and the balance was $7,301. The account had started with $100.
Grok Bot had been checking opportunities, making trading decisions, and sending orders through its connected tools. The screen showed what it owned, why it had entered, and what would make it exit. I could open any trade and follow the decision back to the information available at the time.
That is the part I want to show you how to build.
By the end of this course, you will know how to assemble and check a terminal where Grok Bot runs the trading workflow: finding a setup, deciding whether to take it, opening the position, managing it, and recording the outcome. You will also know how to test each part before allowing it to operate unattended.
Save this before connecting a trading account. Work through it with one market and one strategy.
 
 
1. Start With a Trading Job You Can Explain
My first decision is what the Bot is allowed to trade. Without that, every interesting chart becomes a new strategy and every new strategy needs another set of assumptions.
For this build, use BTC/USD and ETH/USD, hourly candles, long-only spot positions, and one open position at a time. Spot means buying the asset with available cash. We are leaving borrowing and short selling outside the first version.
An hourly approach gives Grok Bot time to inspect the evidence and use its tools. Trying to make a conversational agent compete for a fraction of a second would require a different architecture.
The terminal will follow one candidate through the entire course. A completed BTC candle breaks above its recent range. The Bot examines it, requests a trade, checks the fill, and later closes the position. We will also follow the same opportunity when data is missing or an order only partially fills.

2. Give Grok Bot the Tools to Do the Job
Grok Bot needs a source of market information, a place to execute orders, and a screen where you can inspect the work.
I use Alpaca’s paper environment for the course because it supports crypto simulation and has a documented Python interface. Live crypto access depends on the account and jurisdiction; paper access alone does not establish live eligibility.
The terminal itself uses Python, SQLite, and Streamlit. Python connects the services. SQLite saves the records on one server. Streamlit turns those records into a private dashboard.
A custom MCP connection gives Grok Bot named tools such as “read account,” “inspect candidate,” and “submit this validated order.” MCP is the connection that lets the Bot operate our software through defined commands. These trading tools are part of the project we build, not features already installed in Grok Bot.
Keep the trading service on an always-on host. Grok Bot can continue in its own cloud environment while your laptop is closed, but the separate service must also remain running to manage positions and talk to the broker.
Broker credentials stay on that service. The Bot receives access to its trading tools without receiving permission to edit limits, deploy new code, or move money out of the account.

5. Let the Bot Place a Trade Within Your Limits
An ENTER decision asks the trading service to prepare an order. The service fetches fresh account and price data, calculates the permitted quantity, and returns an order preview with an expiry. Grok Bot can submit that exact intent while it remains valid.
The Bot chooses whether to trade. The service enforces how much it may buy.
With a $100 allocation, the example 0.5% loss budget is $0.50. Size the position from that budget, the distance to the planned exit, and estimated costs. A separate 25% position cap prevents the calculated trade from consuming too much capital.
Suppose an example asset costs $100, the exit distance is $2, and fees plus slippage allowance add $0.60 per unit. The $0.50 budget permits approximately 0.1923 units, worth $19.23 before rounding. Round down to the venue’s permitted increment. If that falls below its minimum, skip the trade.
For the course baseline, put the initial exit two ATRs below the intended entry, the profit target twice that distance above it, and a time exit after 24 hourly bars. Grok Bot may request an earlier exit with a recorded reason. It cannot widen the original loss distance.
Start entries with bounded immediate-or-cancel limit orders: specify the maximum purchase price and allow a partial fill or no fill. An unfilled opportunity expires instead of automatically becoming a more expensive chase.
Alpaca’s documented crypto order types differ from its equity examples. Verify supported order types, time-in-force, and precision in the adapter. Do not assume a stock bracket-order example works for crypto.

6. Keep Managing the Position When Grok Bot Is Busy
Once our BTC entry fills, the terminal has a new responsibility: managing an open position even when the Bot is not responding.
A small position supervisor tracks the broker’s actual holdings and the approved exit conditions. Grok Bot can inspect the position, choose HOLD, or request an early EXIT. Automatic protection continues independently.
One component must own exits. The Bot sends its request to that supervisor so two processes do not both try to sell the same quantity.
Use supported broker-side protection where tested. A stop-limit has a trigger and a limit price; a fast move can leave it unfilled. The terminal must display that condition. Replacing an exit requires checking the existing order, confirming cancellation where necessary, and reconciling the remaining position.
I separate three controls on the screen:
Pause entries: stop adding exposure while existing protection continues.
Cancel pending entries: request cancellation and verify the resulting broker state.
Request liquidation: close the remaining positions under the preapproved procedure and track execution.
These actions have different consequences. A single unlabeled red button leaves too much to guess.
Run the failure check in paper mode:

Simulate a timed-out order, partial fill, and failed cancellation. Report the broker state, the local state, and any unresolved difference.
Define the daily loss halt using start-of-day UTC strategy equity, adjusted for deposits and withdrawals. Include unrealized losses and fees. When the threshold is reached, stop new entries and follow your approved policy for existing positions. A temporary price recovery must not silently reset the halt.
If the broker or network is unavailable, the system may be unable to close a trade. Record the incident, block additional exposure, and notify the owner through an independent monitoring channel.

9. Check Whether the Strategy Deserves Real Money
Before promoting this setup, answer two separate questions: does the trading rule have evidence behind it, and does Grok Bot improve the decisions made from its candidates?
Start with a historical test of the mechanical rule. Divide data chronologically into development, validation, and an untouched final test. Define indicators exactly as the running service does and enter only after the signal candle has closed.
Include fees, spread, slippage, minimum order sizes, unfilled orders, and quantity rounding. If both the stop and target fall inside one hourly candle, use finer data or a conservative assumption; the candle alone cannot establish which happened first.

Then run Grok Bot in shadow mode: it makes decisions on current candidates, but those decisions do not place orders. Save them before observing the outcome. Compare the Bot’s choices with taking every mechanically valid candidate under the same execution assumptions.
This comparison tells you whether its research helps or simply rejects trades unpredictably. Historical model replays can be contaminated by knowledge of later events, which makes prospective records especially valuable.
Finally, run the complete paper workflow and the failure tests. Paper execution establishes whether the pieces communicate correctly. It does not reproduce every live fill, queue position, or market-impact effect.
The order is read-only → shadow decisions → complete paper trading → separately authorized live deployment. Keep live credentials and the activation control outside the Bot’s toolset. Deploy services with persistent storage, restart policies, backups, and an external health monitor.
Move forward only when you can explain both the strategy results and the operational behavior. If the rule loses after costs, the next task is improving or rejecting the rule.
 
10. Read the Result After All the Costs
Our example BTC position now reaches an exit. The supervisor acts, the broker confirms the fill, and the terminal updates the journal. Grok Bot can review what happened using the same evidence that produced the entry.
Read a winning trade, a losing trade, and a rejected candidate together. Did the system follow the approved strategy in each case? Did a correct decision arrive too late? Did execution costs erase a small price advantage?
Here is a worked calculation. A $20 entry sold for $20.40 earns $0.40 before fees. At an assumed 0.25% fee on each side, the two fees total about $0.101, leaving $0.299 before other effects. A $19.60 exit instead leaves about a $0.499 loss after the same fee assumption.
Use the account’s actual fee schedule. Alpaca’s published lowest crypto volume tier lists 15 basis points for maker execution and 25 for taker execution. Reconcile estimated fees against account activities; fees may be charged in the acquired asset and appear later.
The account-level calculation is:
Trading P&L = current equity − starting equity − net deposits.
Then subtract attributable hosting, data, and AI costs to obtain the operating result. Do not subtract fees twice if they already reduced account equity.
To improve that result, reduce work that adds no information. Send Grok Bot a current snapshot and relevant evidence instead of the full archive. Save research with timestamps and refresh it when its validity changes. Reject duplicate candidates by symbol, strategy version, and bar time.
Keep retries specific. A failed data request can retry with backoff; an invalid request needs correction; an uncertain order needs reconciliation. Treating all three as “try again” creates both expense and trading errors.
For scale, checking a model every minute creates 1,440 possible calls per day. A hypothetical day with 12 qualifying candidate reviews has 120 times fewer entry-review calls. Position reviews, input sizes, and retries still affect actual usage. Track what your account exposes and mark unavailable cost measurements as unknown.
