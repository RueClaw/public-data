I've designed the AI brain for consulting firms
Most consulting firms are running on individual laptops with a SharePoint they pretend to use. That is not sustainable. Client data governance requirements are tightening. AI is exposing how little institutional knowledge most firms actually have. The competitive gap is going to widen fast.
The model wars are mostly settled. The real competition now is over who builds the harness on top — and who survives the data governance reckoning that is coming whether the industry is ready for it or not.
I run a 30-person consulting firm. Over the past few weeks we have been designing and have started building what we are calling the Brain. Here is the problem we are solving for, and how we are doing it.
Consulting is supposed to be a learning business. Every engagement teaches the firm something. Every problem solved becomes a pattern. But that only happens when the firm actually captures it. And almost no one does.
The Teams threads where decisions get made. The client calls where scope shifts. The SharePoint folders full of deliverables that took months to perfect. The CRM notes about why a deal closed or didn't. All of it lives somewhere. None of it is connected. So new hires take six months to ramp instead of six weeks. Sales cannot answer "have we done this before" without three meetings. Delivery quality varies based on who happens to be staffed rather than the collective capability of the firm.
This is the consulting firm's version of what I have been calling context amnesia.
Most firms are even further behind than they realize
Before I started my consulting firm I worked at a large one. When I joined, there were no centralized templates. Every proposal was custom. If you needed a starting point you asked around until someone sent you the deck they used last time, which they had built from a deck someone sent them.
Across thousands of consultants. None of it in a system.
This is still the norm at most firms. Even the ones with SharePoint and a knowledge management committee. The official systems exist. The actual work happens in individual OneDrive folders and local files.
That is not just a productivity problem. It is a data governance problem that will eventually kill some of these firms. Client data on individual devices. No version control. No retention policies. No audit trail when someone leaves and takes their laptop with them.
What we are building
We have started calling it the Brain. Three layers, each serving a different purpose.
The first layer indexes everything we already produce. The Teams threads, the SharePoint documents, the call transcripts, the CRM activity. Modern AI search platforms have native connectors to all of it. You point them at your existing tools and the index builds itself. No new platforms to adopt. No new behaviors required from the team. The signal already exists. You are just connecting it.
The second layer is per-practitioner context. Every team member gets a personalized brief rebuilt every night, weighted by the specific roles they hold on each active project. Someone holding Engagement Manager and Lead Consultant on one project gets both delivery and commercial signal for that engagement. The Brain knows what each person needs to know before they ask.
The third layer is action. The Brain does not just surface information. It creates and assigns tasks when it detects gaps. It posts a daily directive to each practitioner every morning, ranked by urgency. It flags scope creep before it becomes a crisis. The human stays in authority. Every Brain action is reviewable and reversible. But the system acts first and explains transparently rather than waiting for permission.
The compounding piece
When a project closes, an AI agent extracts an anonymized knowledge record. Personal names get stripped. Client names stay. The decisions made, the integrations built, the problems encountered, the lessons learned — all preserved permanently in a queryable knowledge base.
After a year, the Brain has 100 closed projects worth of distilled delivery intelligence. After three years, several hundred. That corpus is queryable in plain language. "What approaches have worked for this type of engagement in financial services?" gets a synthesized answer drawn from every relevant project the firm has ever done. The firm gets smarter every week without anyone making it smarter on purpose.
That is the moat. Not the technology. The accumulated intelligence the technology preserves.
This is the most important thing my firm will build in the next decade. If you are running a consulting firm and thinking about the same problem, I want to compare notes.
