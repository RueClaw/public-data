## Article text

Last week the idea of Weight-Space Programming was introduce in the MIT blog post. Since then it has went viral with people pointing out limitations and exploring what is possible with the idea.
It seems like a great idea to explore if community of people can together push an existing model further in capabilities and so last Weds 11/4, we launched Delta League: anyone can submit deltas to improve Qwen 3.5 0.8B by editing the weights with an LLM. 
In one week, 19 people have together improved performance from 17.4% to 53.4% on a private test set that measures math, reasoning, ability to answer on scientific questions, coding and terminal usage.

We are entering a new paradigm. LLMs directly editing weights and even potentially the model architecture without much compute or training data is a game changer. What’s cool about this is: not only do these dramatic improvements validate the experiment, but the community has now turned a very small, less powerful model (800M parameters) into something potentially quite useful: it’s small enough to run locally and it’s inexpensive to use. When training was the only means to edit a model, you would need a large cluster of GPUs, a lab and a team of researchers, data pipelines, and all of this is very expensive and time-consuming.
There is an opportunity now to do open research on LLMs in a more accessible way. In this environment, anyone can compete, whether small groups or even individuals – you don’t need to be an expert or a researcher at a big lab to make these improvements. And you don’t need to be at the frontier to get better performance: you can take a targeted, experimental, and even creative approach with all kinds of models to optimize for something you want. 

Delta League itself is a platform for apple-to-apples model comparison, effectively verifiable eval-as-a-service. Anyone can come up with a model, but how do you trust the results? You need to set up a private evaluation set that no one has seen and which is not available on the internet, and you need to run it on a model without revealing this eval to the model developer.
NEAR AI’s approach of running private and verifiable inference provides a neutral ground where test sets and weights can be uploaded privately and compared. We could see a whole ecosystem and economy start to bloom around companies providing their internal test sets and placing bounties for people to improve models for that company’s specific use cases – while keeping their data private and reducing AI costs.
We’re going to upload the resulting model to Hugging Face to make it available to everyone. We are also planning to launch the next competition on Delta League soon, so stay tuned: https://deltaleague.ai/
