$11,332 a month for a US software developer at the median salary. $100 a month for a Grok Bot plan.
Send it a task from your phone, close your laptop, and come back to review the result. Its cloud computer can keep working overnight, within your plan's usage limits.
Start with something you've been putting off: competitor research, a messy spreadsheet, a small internal tool. Give it the files, explain what you need, and make it show its work.
Here's what to delegate, five prompts to copy, and two reusable skills to make the next assignment easier.
1. Give it a job with a finish line
"Help me with my business" leaves too much for the bot to guess.
"Compare these eight competitors, check their current prices, and give me a spreadsheet with sources" gives it something concrete to deliver.
Grok Bot has a persistent cloud computer with a browser, files, and a terminal. It can work across connected services and websites and retain working context between tasks. You provide the materials and access.
A few useful first assignments:
Research competitors and prepare a pricing comparison.
Read invoices and flag duplicate charges or subscriptions worth reviewing.
 
Prepare a meeting brief from documents and previous conversations.
Reproduce a bug in a test environment and collect evidence.
Build a small script that turns an export into a report.
 
Choose something you already understand. You'll spot mistakes faster and know whether the bot saved you time.
For coding tasks, describe the desired behavior in plain English. Give it an example input and show what the output should look like. You don't need to know the implementation to get started, although consequential code still needs someone who can review it properly.
2. Hand out work from your phone
You're away from your desk and remember that tomorrow's meeting needs a competitor brief.
 
Open Grok Bot on your iPhone or Android and send the assignment. The mobile app connects to the same Bots and conversations you use on desktop.
Compare the pricing pages for these four companies before tomorrow's meeting. Save a spreadsheet with prices, billing terms, and source links. Flag anything that requires contacting sales. Leave me a short summary in this chat.

Focus on plans for a team of ten. Include annual commitments and minimum seat requirements.
The cloud task can continue after you close the app. If it needs your input, return to the conversation from your phone.
With separate setup and permissions, the bot can also run commands and scripts on your own computer. Don't confuse that with unrestricted remote desktop control. For work that runs entirely in the cloud, your laptop can stay closed.
3. What has it actually done?
The useful examples show the inputs, the work, and the result. You should be able to follow what happened.
Reviewing procurement costs
In its Haggle Bot case study, the company behind Grok Bot describes giving an agent access to vendor spending, contracts, and usage data. It compared alternatives and prepared negotiation material.
The company reports that one purchasing workflow reduced a tech order from $14,629 to $6,143. It also says the bot identified more than $100,000 in direct savings opportunities.
 
These are vendor-reported results. People still edited the bot's emails and approved consequential actions.
You could try a smaller version with your own subscriptions: what you're paying, what your team actually uses, and what can change at renewal. The opportunity depends on your bills and contract terms.
Reproducing bugs and preparing sales work
The Grok Bot launch article describes an internal engineering workflow where a bot reproduces a bug in the product interface, files a ticket, and hands the fix to another bot.
It also describes updating CRM records from call notes and researching accounts overnight to prepare outreach drafts.
For your own trial, ask for intermediate evidence: steps you can repeat for a bug report, and sources you can open for an account brief.
 
Building interactive projects
The official Grok Build showcase includes a forest driving game and an isometric city simulator, alongside the prompts used to create them.
These belong to Grok Build, a separate product from Grok Bot. They show examples of interactive prototypes in that environment; they don't establish that Grok Bot built those projects.
Try the demos, look for bugs, and consider what would still be needed before real users depended on them.
4. Decide what "done" means
An AI can produce a convincing explanation of work that isn't finished. Give it something you can check.
For an app, require a working launch and a check of the main user flow. For a spreadsheet, require totals that reconcile with the input. For research, require sources that support the claims.
Take a small CSV tool. Before implementation, decide:
Which columns does it accept?
What happens when a required column is missing?
What file should the user receive?
You now have acceptance criteria.
The company's IncidentSweep example makes the review step visible. The bot presents the selected tickets and exact reply for approval before sending a batch. The authors report clearing 204 tickets in about ten minutes with a support engineer reviewing and approving the work.
For your own trial, measure how much remains after the bot finishes. A good result on a specific task tells you something useful. It doesn't prove the bot makes fewer mistakes than every programmer.
 
5. Five prompts worth keeping
Replace the bracketed fields with your information. The more clearly you define the output, the easier it becomes to judge the result.
Prompt 1: Hand over a complete assignment
 
Spend an extra minute defining the deliverable.
"A report" is vague. "A spreadsheet with one row per vendor, monthly spend, renewal date, and a source for each figure" is something you can inspect.
Prompt 2: Research a decision
 
Use this when the result will influence a purchase or product decision. Check the sources behind the recommendation before acting on it.
Prompt 3: Build a small working tool
 
Keep the first version small enough to test yourself. Add features after the basic workflow works.
Prompt 4: Review the result before I use it
 
A separate review gives the task another pass. It still needs evidence. Asking the bot to "be extremely critical" isn't much of a test by itself.
Prompt 5: Prepare an overnight assignment
 
A deadline in a message doesn't create a schedule. Use this to hand over work now. Set up a routine separately if you want it repeated.

8. Check whether you're getting your evenings back
After a few assignments, count your own time.
Include preparing the request, answering questions, reviewing the result, and fixing anything the bot missed. Include extra usage charges and other services required.
 
Suppose a weekly report used to take three hours. With the bot, you spend fifteen minutes preparing the inputs and twenty-five minutes reviewing the output.
If the quality holds up, you've freed two hours and twenty minutes. This is an example calculation, not a measured Grok Bot result.
If you spend three hours repairing its work, the workflow needs attention. Expand it after you can get a reliable result on the smaller task. The company's customer support rollout followed that gradual approach: begin with review, inspect the results, and increase responsibility as the process improves.

9. Give it one job tonight
Find a task you've postponed at least twice. Pick one where you have the necessary inputs and can recognize a good result.
Use the first prompt. Attach the files. Write down the acceptance criteria before the bot begins.
Watch the first attempt closely enough to catch missing access or a bad assumption. Once the process works, save it as a skill and try again with fresh inputs.
Then try leaving it overnight.
 
In the morning, open the deliverable and check it against the assignment. Keep track of how much work is left for you. That will tell you far more about the value of your $100 subscription than the number of messages the bot sent while you were asleep.
