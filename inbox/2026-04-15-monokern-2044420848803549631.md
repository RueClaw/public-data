## Article text

You've been apartment hunting for 3 weeks. You've seen 12 places. Number 9 was genuinely great - good price, great location. You passed. Now you're seeing #13 and it's mediocre. You're starting to wonder if #9 was the one.
That feeling has a name. And more importantly - there's an exact mathematical answer to when you should have committed.
It's called the Secretary Problem. And once you understand it, you'll never make this type of decision the same way again.
 
The Setup
Here's the classic version:
You're hiring a secretary. N candidates, interviewed one by one, random order. After each interview - you must decide immediately: hire or reject. No callbacks. If you reach the end without hiring anyone, you take the last one. Goal: maximize your probability of hiring the best candidate.
Same logic applies everywhere:
Apartment hunting in a competitive city
Picking the best entry point on Polymarket when a new narrative drops and odds are constantly shifting
Finding the best deal while walking past shops
Choosing a long-term partner (yes, this math applies there too - it's literally called the "marriage problem" in some papers)
Looking for a better job offer
In all of tпеhese: you can't go back. You decide now or lose the option forever.
 
Why Random Guessing Destroys You
The naive approach: just pick whoever feels right. Or always pick the first one. Or always pick the last one.
All of these have the same terrible success rate: P = 1/N
3 candidates → 33% chance of picking the best
10 candidates → 10% chance
100 candidates → 1% chance
The more options you have, the more randomness punishes you. This is unacceptable.
Is there something better?
 
 
The Only Thing You Can Control Is When You Stop Rejecting
Here's the key insight that makes this problem solvable.
You can't control who shows up or in what order. The only lever you have is: how many people you deliberately reject before you start seriously considering.
The strategy works like this:
Interview the first R candidates. Reject all of them. But track the best score you've seen - call it X_ref
From candidate R+1 onward - hire the first one who beats X_ref
The problem reduces to one question: what's the optimal R?
Too small R → your benchmark is garbage, you'll commit to a mediocre candidate early
Too large R → you risk rejecting the best candidate during the "observation phase"
There's a mathematically precise answer.
 
The Math (Don't Skip This - It's Worth It)
We need to find P(R): the probability of successfully picking the best candidate given that we rejected the first R people.
Step 1. 
Break the problem down. P(R) is the sum of probabilities that the n-th candidate is both chosen by our rule AND is actually the best overall - for every possible position n from R+1 to N:
P(R) = \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} P(\text{choose } n^{th} \text{ and } n^{th} \text{ is the best one})

Step 2. 
How do we compute that inner probability? Two elegant approaches lead to the same answer.
Approach A - think about the 2nd-best candidate:
The n-th candidate gets selected AND is the best overall, exactly when the second-best candidate was rejected in the first R slots (because that's what made our benchmark high enough to skip everyone between R and n).
 
In terms of math, this looks like:
P(R) = \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} P(n^{th} \text{ best}) \cdot P(2^{nd} \text{ best was in first } R \text{ out of } n-1) =
= \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{N} \cdot \frac{R}{n-1} = \frac{R}{N} \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{n-1}

Approach B - think about record scores:
Imagine flipping a special coin for each candidate from R+1 to N. The coin decides if that candidate sets a new record. Due to random ordering, a record at position m happens with probability 1/m. We want exactly one record - at position n (our chosen candidate). All others must fail.
 
If we translate this logic into a formula, we get:
P(R) = \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{n} \prod_{m=R+1,\ldots,N,\, m \neq n} \left(1 - \frac{1}{m}\right) =
= \prod_{m=R+1,\ldots,N} \left(1 - \frac{1}{m}\right) \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{n(1 - 1/n)} = \frac{R}{N} \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{n-1}
Both approaches give the same result. When two different paths lead to the same formula - that's math telling you you're on solid ground.
The final formula:
{P(R) = \frac{R}{N} \sum_{n=R+1}^{N} \frac{1}{n-1}}
N = Total number of candidates or options.
R = The number of initial options you reject to establish your baseline (benchmark).
n = The position of the current candidate being evaluated (from R+1 up to N).
P(R)= Your probability of successfully picking the absolute best candidate overall.
The 1/e Law - The Practical Rule
Now you maximize P(R) over all possible values of R.
When you do this numerically (or analytically for large N), something remarkable appears:
The optimal R* converges to exactly N/e ≈ N/2.718 ≈ 0.37 × N
And the maximum success probability P(R*) also converges to 
1/e ≈ 37%
 
Two things that make this result insane:
1. The 37% floor holds regardless of N
With random guessing: 100 candidates → 1% success rate.
With the optimal stopping strategy: 100 candidates → still ~37% success rate. 
The probability doesn't collapse as the problem gets harder. That's asymptotically impossible-sounding but mathematically true.
2. It's better than random even with tiny N
With just N=3 candidates and R=1 (reject the first, then pick the next best): success probability = 50%. Random guessing gives 33%. One simple rule, massive improvement.
 
The Practical Rule
Reject the first 37% of your options. Then commit to the next one that's better than everything you've seen.
Apartment hunting: saw 20 places? Reject the first 7 without committing. Use them to calibrate. Then take the first one after that which beats all 7.
Job offers: Got 10 offers coming in over a month? Let the first 4 pass. Take the next one that's clearly the best you've seen.
Dating? The math literally works here too. If you expect to have ~10 serious relationships in your life - date and learn from the first 3-4 without committing. Then commit to the first person after that who's better than all of them.
Polymarket trading: Watching a new market develop with ~10 potential entry points over a day? Sit out the first 3-4 odds shifts to see how the crowd reacts. Then buy the next dip that gives you a better price than anything you saw in the observation phase.
 
The Uncomfortable Part
Most people never do this. They either commit too early (panic, fear of missing out (FOMO)) or too late (waiting for perfection that doesn't exist).
The Secretary Problem tells you: both extremes are mistakes, and there's an exact middle.
You can't guarantee the best outcome. Sometimes the best candidate shows up in slot 2 and you lose them. That's variance. It happens.
But if you play this strategy across 100 decisions in your life, you'll be right ~37% of the time instead of the 1-10% that pure randomness gives you.
That's not a small edge. That's the difference between good and great decision-making.
Now go set your R
