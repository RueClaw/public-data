Principles of Outcome Engineering

You don’t hand a system a ticket to finish. You state what should remain true, how it will know, and what it may touch — then it keeps that true.

One principle at a time

Eight ideas from the essay, plus a short open and close. Use the dots, the buttons, or the left and right arrow keys.

A new unit of work

The essay is not a product or a process. It is a claim about what we should ask of systems that can already run, check themselves, and keep going.

The old unit

A goal or a task. You start it, someone finishes it, and the work is over.

The proposed unit

An outcome: a statement of what should remain true, kept by the system for as long as you depend on it.

The essay says this is becoming possible because of “proactive, perpetual, self-verifying systems.”

Outcome as the new unit of work

“We as humans provide a statement of what should remain true at the end.”

Goal

“Build this Android app”

Outcome

“Build an Android version of our iOS app and keep it at parity forever”

Both lines are from the essay. The goal ends when the app ships. The outcome stays true only if parity is kept.

Outcomes carry their own proof

“A good outcome says how the system will know it is true.” Writing that definition, the essay says, “sets the ceiling on what the system can deliver” and is the important work people still do by hand.

Task

“Fix the bug from this support ticket”

A target the system can measure itself against

“every support ticket caused by a bug ends in a shipped fix and a reply to the customer”

Both lines are from the essay. The task closes one ticket. The target keeps being true for every later ticket of that kind.

Machinery should be invisible

You bring an outcome. The system should “manage / abstract away everything else.” Like compiled code: you can read what’s underneath, but you rarely need to.

The essay lists these tools and says almost none of them should be our responsibility.

Human attention is for decisions

“People should step in only for judgment calls a human should make. The system should be smart enough to surface these and notify us when an answer is needed.”

Attention is scarce. The essay spends it only on decisions a person should make.

“Done” is a state

The system stays responsible “for as long as we depend on the outcome.” It watches for change, keeps the outcome true, and looks for ways to make it better “without requiring anyone to ask.”

“The outcome is the durable asset; the code beneath it can be rewritten as many times as it takes.”

Ambition is unbound

“The most challenging part of a big idea was never starting it, it was finishing it and maintaining it forever. Now that our systems can do both we can be more ambitious.”

What used to cap the idea

Not the blank page — the finishing, and then keeping it alive forever.

What the essay unlocks

If a system can finish and maintain, the size of the idea is no longer limited by that old cost.

This is an argument, not a measurement. The next principle is the safety rail that comes with it.

Authority is bound

“A system that works perpetually and proactively needs a clear limit on what it may touch. We state what the system is allowed to do alongside what we want, then we can leave it alone.”

Ambition and authority arrive as a pair. A perpetual system without a limit cannot safely be left alone.

Experimentation as a first principle

“Every open question becomes something we can just test. A wrong guess is no longer an opportunity cost because the system can be wrong N times on the way to being right (and in parallel).”

The essay treats failed tries as cheap if they can run together. It does not discuss cost or blast radius.

Forget the machinery exists

“Outcome engineering is where we fully give in to our imagination, embrace ambition, and forget that the underlying system and machinery exist.”

The essay’s close

This way of building is “now becoming possible due to the emergence of proactive, perpetual, self-verifying systems.” The author writes that it is “already being embraced by some of the best engineers I know” and “will redefine software engineering going into 2027.”

That last part is a prediction and an anecdote, not evidence. Next, try writing an outcome yourself, then check what you kept.

About this essay

A short review of the source, after the walkthrough.

📚 Good reference: a vocabulary, not a method

Nader Dabit’s page is eight principles and two contrasts, not a framework with steps, a product, or a case study. The useful move is to write what should remain true, how the system will know, and what it may touch. The 2027 prediction and the “best engineers I know” line are anecdotal.
